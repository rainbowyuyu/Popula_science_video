Introduction


The neutrino was proposed by W. Pauli (awarded the Nobel Prize in Physics, 1945, “for the
discovery of the Exclusion Principle, also called the Pauli Principle”) in 1930 to explain the
continuous beta-decay spectrum observed for radioactive elements. Experimental confirmation
was provided in 1956 by C. Cowan and F. Reines using a nuclear reactor as a potent source of
antielectron neutrinos. In 1995, Reines was awarded the Nobel Prize in Physics “for the detection
of the neutrino” (Cowan had passed away in 1974).


In 1988, L.M. Lederman, M. Schwartz and J. Steinberger were awarded the Nobel Prize in Physics
“for the neutrino beam method and the demonstration of the doublet structure of the leptons
through the discovery of the muon neutrino”. It is now known that there are three neutrino
flavour eigenstates: 𝜈𝜈𝑒𝑒 , 𝜈𝜈𝜇𝜇 and 𝜈𝜈𝜏𝜏 . These are superpositions of three mass eigenstates, where the
sub-eV masses and mass ordering are unknown. In 2015, the Nobel Prize in Physics was awarded
to T. Kajita and A.B. McDonald “for the discovery of neutrino oscillations, which shows that
neutrinos have mass”.


Once discovered by Cowan and Reines, the neutrino was immediately understood to be an ideal
astronomical messenger, since it is electrically neutral and only subject to weak or gravitational
interactions. This means that the neutrino can provide an unobstructed view of dense celestial
sources without being absorbed or magnetically deflected within the source or en route to the
Earth. In 2002, R. Davis Jr. and M. Koshiba were awarded the Nobel Prize in Physics “for
pioneering contributions to astrophysics, in particular for the detection of cosmic neutrinos”
after the detection of megaelectronvolt (MeV, 106 eV) neutrinos from the Sun and the 160,000-
light-year distant supernova 1987A.


This year, the Nobel Prize in Physics recognises the discovery of a diffuse flux of astrophysical
neutrinos, which extends to petaelectronvolt (PeV, 1015 eV) energies. Such neutrinos probe

Francis Halzen has long been the driving force behind the IceCube Neutrino Observatory. As
Principal Investigator from its beginnings, he has spearheaded the collective work by scientists
and engineers that resulted in a volume of instrumented Antarctic ice sufficiently large to detect
high-energy neutrinos of astrophysical origin. His scientific vision thus provides the basis for new
research in the future of this field.



Neutrino production in cosmic accelerators


Cosmic rays were discovered in 1912 by V.F. Hess (Nobel Prize in Physics 1936). Since then, the
cosmic radiation has been extensively studied and is now known to comprise primarily protons
and helium nuclei, which continuously impinge on the Earth’s atmosphere [1].


The cosmic-ray energy spectrum is measured up to ~1020 eV – an energy which greatly exceeds
that in terrestrial accelerator beams [e.g., 7 TeV (7×1012 eV) at the CERN Large Hadron Collider].
The energy spectrum can be described by a broken power law, where a spectral break at a few
petaelectronvolts is referred to as the “knee”. Up to this energy, cosmic rays are believed to
originate within the Milky Way with centurial supernova explosions responsible for the
acceleration.


Around a few exaelectronvolts (EeV, 1018 eV), a second feature called the “ankle” marks where the
gyroradius of a proton exceeds the galactic scale, and an extragalactic component becomes
dominant. The exaelectronvolt flux is small – roughly one cosmic-ray particle per year reaches a
square kilometre on the Earth's surface. Above ~50 EeV, the cosmic-ray flux rapidly diminishes
as cosmic rays interact with cosmic microwave background photons – the GZK (Greisen-
Zatsepin-Kuzmin) cut-off.




2 (18)
As outlined in the Appendix, extragalactic cosmic rays may be accelerated within an active galactic
nucleus (AGN). In an AGN, matter is accreted onto a supermassive black hole lying at the centre
of a galaxy, which releases gravitational energy. A fraction of this energy powers two collimated
jets of relativistic plasma, which are launched in opposite directions along the rotation axis,
perpendicular to the plane of the accretion disk. The jets propagate outwards for up to several
hundred thousand light years, until they terminate against the intergalactic medium.


At these termination shock fronts, as well as those within the jet, the kinetic energy of the outflow
is converted into particle acceleration. Particles are repeatedly scattered back and forth across
shock fronts, successively gaining energy (so-called first-order Fermi acceleration).


The Hillas criterion [2] must be met; i.e., the magnetic field strength and physical size of the
accelerating region must together be sufficient to confine and accelerate a particle to the required
energy before its gyroradius becomes too large and the particle escapes. Identification of
acceleration sites using terrestrial observations of high-energy cosmic rays is complicated by the
significant angular smearing caused by intervening magnetic fields [3].

Unlike charged cosmic rays, neutrinos are not deflected by galactic and intergalactic magnetic
fields, and unlike gamma-rays, they can escape optically thick sources without subsequent
attenuation caused by interactions with the extragalactic background light or cosmic microwave
background. Moreover, gamma-rays can also be generated by electrons in synchrotron or inverse
Compton processes, which complicates source diagnosis. In the burgeoning field of multi-
messenger astronomy, information gleaned from high-energy neutrinos, ultra-high–energy
cosmic rays, gamma-rays and, potentially, gravitational waves are combined with the aim of
providing a complete picture of cosmic accelerators.

Detecting high-energy neutrinos on Earth


At teraelectronvolt energies and above, neutrino interactions are dominated by deep inelastic
scattering. As shown in Figure 2, a neutrino weakly interacts with quarks in the nucleons (N) of
the detection medium. A charged-current interaction,


𝜈𝜈𝑙𝑙 + 𝑁𝑁 → 𝑙𝑙 − + X,


results in a charged lepton (l-), and a cascade of particles (X) from the disintegrated nucleus. A
neutral-current interaction,


𝜈𝜈𝑙𝑙 + 𝑁𝑁 → 𝜈𝜈𝑙𝑙 + X,


results in a cascade alone.


Cherenkov light is created when the charged lepton or cascade particles traverse a dielectric
medium with refractive index, n, at a speed, v, which exceeds the phase velocity of light in the
medium; i.e., v>c/n, where c is the speed of light in a vacuum. In water (n=1.33 at 20°C), the
Cherenkov emission threshold is 0.75c, which corresponds to an energy threshold of 160 MeV for
a muon. Neutrino telescopes identify interacting neutrinos by detecting this UV-dominated
Cherenkov light.


The magnitude of the petaelectronvolt neutrino–nucleon interaction cross-section (~10-33 cm2),
combined with the steeply falling neutrino energy spectrum expected from astrophysical sources
(a typical power law index is −2.5), means that a cubic-kilometre–scale detector is required to
achieve practical detection rates. The use of large volumes of liquid water as a Cherenkov medium
was independently proposed in 1960 by M. Markov [5], F. Reines [6] and K. Greisen [7]. In 1975,
Reines and J. Learned initiated influential workshops, which resulted in plans to establish a
kilometre-scale detector by deploying “strings” of optical sensors (photomultiplier tubes, PMTs)
anchored to the sea floor.


The approach was pioneered by the DUMAND project at a depth of 4800 m off the coast of
Hawaii. One string was deployed in 1993, but suffered technical problems and failed after a short
time. Moreover, measurement backgrounds from ocean bioluminescence and 40K were identified,
which affected measurement sensitivity. The project was cancelled in 1995, but its technological

Neutrino detection in ice


During the autumn of 1987, Halzen gave a talk at the University of Kansas. A glaciologist in the
audience, E. Zeller, informed Halzen about Russian plans to detect radio emission from charged
particles produced in neutrino interactions in the Antarctic ice. Halzen, together with colleagues
T. Stanev and E. Zas, deduced that the detection threshold would be too high for petaelectronvolt
astrophysical neutrinos.




6 (18)
Halzen’s thoughts turned to neutrino detection via Cherenkov light. The idea to establish a
neutrino detector with ice as both the target and Cherenkov radiator medium was first reported
[12] in June 1988 by Halzen and Learned. In a popular science article, Halzen reminisced [13]:


              “I suspect that others must have contemplated the same idea and given up on it.
              Had I not been completely ignorant of what was then known about the optical
              properties of natural ice, I would probably have done the same. Instead, I sent a
              flurry of E-mail messages to my friend John G. Learned, then the spokesperson
              for the Deep Underwater Muon and Neutrino Detector (DUMAND).
              …
              Learned immediately appreciated the advantages of an Antarctic neutrino
              telescope.”


Learned continued to focus on leading the development of the DUMAND project, while Halzen
devoted his career to the development of ice-based neutrino detection – a journey which
eventually led to the IceCube Neutrino Observatory [14].


A milestone on the way to a large-scale neutrino detector was reported in 1991. Photomultiplier
tubes placed into existing boreholes on the Greenland icesheet [15] were used to detect the
Cherenkov light produced within the ice by cosmic-ray muons created in the atmosphere. The
measurements showed that the ice was sufficiently transparent to Cherenkov light, which
informed discussions of a kilometre-scale detector [16].


The Antarctic Muon and Neutrino Detection Array (AMANDA) [17] subsequently demonstrated
the instrumentation approach eventually used for IceCube, whereby strings of photomultiplier
tubes are deployed into kilometre-deep holes in the ice. The holes were drilled using a high-
pressure hot water system originally pioneered for glaciology. AMANDA was constructed in a
number of phases between 1993 and 2000. Halzen was a driving force on AMANDA.


An important discovery was that 50-𝜇𝜇m air bubbles trapped in the ice at the initial deployment
depth (800 m to 1000 m) resulted in a scattering length for Cherenkov light of <50 cm. This
hampered the reconstruction of neutrino trajectories, motivating subsequent string deployment
between 1500 m and 2000 m. In March 2001, results on atmospheric neutrinos (50 GeV to a few
TeV) were published [18], demonstrating the feasibility of large-scale ice-based neutrino
detection. The AMANDA Collaboration went on to publish a broad range of results [19], including

limits on the magnitude of the astrophysical neutrino flux. AMANDA was decommissioned in

The IceCube Neutrino Observatory


Building on the success of AMANDA, a proposal to establish the IceCube Neutrino Observatory
was submitted to the NSF in 1999, with Halzen as Principal Investigator. The NSF approved
funding in March 2002, and construction started in 2004. IceCube [20] was established in the
Antarctic ice close to the Amundsen-Scott South Pole Station.


The Cherenkov light signal is detected using strings of 60 optical sensors, each comprising a 25.4-
cm-diameter downward-facing hemispherical photomultiplier tube with integrated digitizing
electronics, deployed over a kilometre deep every 17 m. Photomultiplier data are sent to the
surface through optical fibres. Using the hot water drilling technique, a total of 86 strings were
deployed from 2005 to 2010, at depths between 1450 m and 2450 m, in a hexagonal array with a
triangular grid spacing of 125 m. The resulting cubic kilometre of ice (a target mass of ~1 Gt)
instrumented with over 5000 optical sensors is shown schematically in Figure 3.


Below a depth of ~2100 m, where the ice is clearest, the absorption (scattering) length for
Cherenkov light is ~200 m (~50 m). The refractive index of the ice is ~1.31, with birefringent
properties that were recently revealed [21].


An array of surface-ice Cherenkov detectors (“IceTop”) located above IceCube measures cosmic-
ray air showers in the 300-TeV to 1-EeV energy range. IceTop provides a “veto” for in-ice events
over a limited solid angle.


Since completion in 2011, IceCube has monitored the neutrino sky with an uptime exceeding 99%.
Neutrino interactions recorded by IceCube have two primary topologies – tracks and cascades, as
shown in Figure 4. The number of detected Cherenkov photons is used to determine the deposited
energy, while the relative arrival times of photons determines the neutrino direction.


A muon neutrino that interacts through a charged-current process will produce a muon, which
may traverse IceCube leaving a track-like signature. Muon neutrinos are detected even when they
interact far outside of the IceCube volume, since a teraelectronvolt muon has a kilometre range

The neutrino trajectory can be reconstructed with an angular resolution of 0.3° at 100 TeV. The
energy of individual muons can be determined within a factor of ~2 due to the stochastic nature
of the muon energy loss (e.g., ionisation, bremsstrahlung, photo-ionisation). The neutrino energy
spectrum is unfolded from the deposited energy distribution.


Cascades arise from charged-current electron and tau neutrino interactions, as well as neutral-
current interactions of all neutrino flavours. The resulting electromagnetic or hadronic shower is
typically less than ~10 m in length, which is smaller than the distance between strings, resulting
in a spherical pattern of reconstructed Cherenkov light. This means that the angular resolution is

worse than for tracks, at around 5° for energies above 50 TeV. Since the interaction event is well-
contained, the energy resolution is ~8% at 100 TeV, i.e., far superior to that for tracks.


Tau neutrinos that undergo a charged-current interaction in the ice produce a so-called “double-
bang” signature [22], with two spatially separated cascades – one from the tau neutrino
interaction and the other from decay of the resulting tau lepton. With a tau decay length of ~50
m/PeV, detecting such events requires a favourable event geometry. IceCube instead detects tau
neutrinos by identifying characteristic light patterns across multiple optical modules and strings
as each cascade passes by.

To identify high-energy astrophysical neutrino interactions, two measurement backgrounds need
to be suppressed during data analysis. With regard to the first background suppression, cosmic-
ray muons are produced in the decay of pions and kaons in the atmosphere above IceCube. The
muon flux peaks in the zenith direction and decreases as the incidence angle approaches the
horizon. This background is effectively suppressed by selecting tracks that arrive from directions
below the horizon. In a typical analysis (such as that described below), the initial 3-kHz
atmospheric muon background can be reduced to 𝜇𝜇Hz-level in this manner.




10 (18)
The second suppression concerns the approximately 100,000 neutrinos that are registered
annually above ~0.1 TeV. The majority are created in the Earth’s atmosphere by pion and muon
decay. The atmospheric neutrino flux may also contain so-called prompt neutrinos from the decay
of charm mesons. These neutrinos are expected to have a flatter energy spectrum than those from
lighter mesons. Above a few tens of teraelectronvolts, the astrophysical neutrino flux (~E-2.5)
emerges above the steeply falling atmospheric background flux (~E-3.7). Per year, ~100 neutrinos
are expected to be of astrophysical origin.



IceCube results on high-energy astrophysical neutrinos


In 2013, after two years of data collection, two petaelectronvolt neutrino events (1.04 PeV and 1.14
PeV) were discovered in the data, providing the first evidence for high-energy astrophysical
neutrinos [23]. The two events were found serendipitously [24], while searching for GZK
neutrinos at the exaelectronvolt energy scale using “starting event” data collected between May
2010 and May 2012. The instrumented volume of ice is divided into an outer veto shield and a
~500 megaton inner detection volume. Neutrinos from any direction, which first interact within
the detection volume of IceCube, are referred to as “starting events”. This event selection provides
additional discrimination against atmospheric muon events impinging the detection volume from
the outside.


The first two PeV events informed subsequent data analysis strategy and paved the way for the
discovery of 28 high-energy (30 TeV–1.14 PeV) astrophysical neutrinos in the same dataset [25].
A total of 21 events were cascades, and the remaining 7 were muon tracks. In 2014, published
results [26] rejected a purely atmospheric explanation for data collected 2010–2013 at 5.7σ level.


Since then, IceCube has periodically published updated results on high-energy astrophysical
neutrinos [27]. The most recent results were published in March 2026 [28], including a down-
going muon neutrino with a reconstructed energy of 11.4+2.46
                                                       −2.53 PeV. The neutrino was recorded on

31 March 2019, and the observation is the highest energy neutrino published by IceCube to date.


The all-flavour astrophysical neutrino energy spectrum was measured by analysing the
complementary track and cascade samples of neutrino interactions. A total of 231,486 tracks (8.5
years live time) and 10,569 cascades (10.5 years live time) were recorded between May 2010 and
May 2021. The muon track sample [29] predominantly covers the Northern sky, while the all-
flavour cascade sample [30] covers the entire sky.

A variety of hypotheses on the spectral shape of the high-energy astrophysical neutrino flux have
been examined. There is evidence for a flatter neutrino spectrum below ~30 TeV, while at higher
energies the spectrum is well-described by a power law, as shown in Figure 6. In the range 5 TeV–
10 PeV, a single power law is rejected with a significance exceeding 4σ. Information on the spectral
shape is a powerful input when modelling sources of astrophysical neutrinos, e.g., indicating
transition between contributing source populations or production regions. As expected for a
collection of predominantly extragalactic neutrino sources, no significant anisotropy in the
neutrino arrival direction on the sky is observed.


Figure 6 shows the flux contribution from high-energy neutrinos produced in the Milky Way.
Results on galactic neutrinos were first published in 2023 [31], with a statistical significance of
4.5σ reported. No point sources were identified. The neutrinos are produced when cosmic-ray
protons and nuclei are accelerated within the Milky Way and interact with the interstellar gas.
The signal is consistent with expectations from measurements of diffuse (𝜋𝜋0 decay) gamma-rays
by the Fermi Gamma-ray Space Telescope.


Earlier this year, IceCube released updated results [32], describing an observation of high-energy
galactic neutrinos with a statistical significance of 5.7σ. Based on 12 years of data, this establishes
the Milky Way as the first source of high-energy neutrinos where the 5σ discovery threshold is
exceeded. Once again, no point sources were identified. The flux contribution from a possible
point source of neutrinos, NGC 1068 – an active galactic nucleus, which is discussed in the
Appendix – is also shown. Both the galactic and point source neutrino fluxes are small compared
to the diffuse astrophysical neutrino flux.

The measured neutrino energy flux is comparable to that observed for gamma-rays at lower
energies, and cosmic rays at higher energies. This suggests that these particles are accelerated by
the same class of sources, through related physical processes. Hadronic interactions are natural
candidates: i.e., a source accelerates protons or nuclei (the ultra-high-energy cosmic rays
measured by, e.g., the Pierre Auger Observatory); some of the protons or nuclei interact with
matter or radiation in or near the source, to produce pions; the neutral pions decay into the
gamma-rays seen by the Fermi Gamma-ray Space Telescope; and the charged pions decay into
the neutrinos, which IceCube measures. In this way, all three messengers are tied together in
terms of energy output, as discussed earlier.



Summary and outlook


A diffuse flux of astrophysical neutrinos extending to petaelectronvolt energies (1015 eV) has been
discovered using data from the IceCube Neutrino Observatory. High-energy neutrinos are
thought to be produced in the interactions of cosmic-ray protons and nuclei at celestial
acceleration sites. The neutrinos deliver undistorted directional and spectral information, thereby
opening a new observation window on the high-energy universe. IceCube results demonstrate
that sources are predominately extragalactic.


Francis Halzen has made pivotal contributions to IceCube as Principal Investigator – from its
invention and conceptual design, through the technically audacious construction, to routine
science operations. Through his visionary leadership, Halzen created and nurtured an
international collaboration, which was able to transform theoretical ideas on neutrino detection
into precise observations and a vibrant experimental field.


The use of high-energy neutrinos in multi-messenger astronomy will be fully capitalised on during
the next decade with a global network of next-generation neutrino telescopes with instrumented
volumes exceeding a cubic-kilometre. These facilities include the IceCube-Gen2 Observatory
(South Pole), KM3NeT (Toulon/Sicily, Mediterranean Sea), P-ONE (Vancouver Island, Pacific
Ocean), TRIDENT (South China Sea), and Baikal-GVD (Lake Baikal, Siberia).



Appendix: Sources of high-energy astrophysical neutrinos


IceCube has presented results showing that the reconstructed trajectories of high-energy
astrophysical neutrinos can be traced back to an active galactic nucleus (AGN). An AGN is the


15 (18)
extremely bright central region of a galaxy. The emission arises from dust and gas, which falls into
a rotating supermassive black hole, resulting in a collimated outflow of highly relativistic material,
referred to as a “jet”. The jet is thought to be formed when the accretion disk spacetime
experiences the black hole, and the disk magnetic field is wound into a tight cone around the
rotation axis, which acts to funnel a jet of highly relativistic particles. If the jet is directed to Earth,
the AGN is referred to as a blazar. It remains unclear if the acceleration power stems from the
rotational energy of the black hole or the magnetic energy in the rotating plasma. When the
hadronic jet impacts target material, e.g., the coronal UV-photon field or the gas or dust, neutrinos
are produced. The neutrino-AGN connection stems from the 1990s [34].


On 22 September 2017, IceCube detected a ~290-TeV muon neutrino (180 TeV deposited energy)
from a direction within 0.06° of the sky position of the blazar TXS 0506+056 [35]. The source
was “flaring”, with a gamma-ray flux that had increased by a factor of 7 in recent months. Within
a minute of the neutrino detection, an alert was distributed worldwide, prompting follow-up
searches by telescopes over a broad range of wavelengths. Analysis of archived IceCube data [36]
revealed a neutrino flare from September 2014 to March 2015, a 3.5σ detection.


High-energy neutrino emission from positions of known gamma-ray emitting sources has been
found in archived IceCube data. After searching 110 such positions for a neutrino excess, IceCube
announced in 2022 that 79 neutrinos in the energy range 1–10 TeV had been observed from the
direction of the AGN NGC 1068 [37] [also known as Messier 77 (M77)], a detection over
background of 4.2σ. NGC 1068 is among the brightest and closest AGN, lying in the constellation
of Cetus, 46 million light-years from Earth. The AGN is radio-quiet with no discernible large-scale
jet activity. Observations by the Atacama Large Millimeter/submillimeter Array (ALMA) and the
X-ray space telescope NuSTAR [38] indicate that the 15-million-solar-mass super-massive black
hole at the centre of the galaxy is blanketed by a thick layer of gas and dust. The gamma-ray energy
flux measured by the Fermi Gamma-ray Space Telescope (0.1–30 GeV) is much less than the
neutrino energy flux, and the MAGIC imaging atmospheric Cherenkov telescope did not detect
any teraelectronvolt emission. The combination of these observations favours neutrino
production through pion decay in the radiation-dense corona surrounding the central black hole.
Time will tell if these observations confirm AGNs as sources of high-energy neutrinos.


Finally, gamma-ray bursts (GRBs) have also been proposed as a source of high-energy
astrophysical neutrinos [39]. To complete the picture, it is worth noting that more than a
thousand follow-up observations of GRBs (based on sky position and time) have been conducted,
without any coincident detection of neutrinos.
